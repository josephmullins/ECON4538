Assignment 4

This assignment studies how intergenerational mobility is measured, building on Lecture 4. Questions 1 and 2 use simulated data, so you know the true answer and can see exactly how measurement problems bias estimates. Question 3 uses the commuting zone (CZ) data from Chetty et al. (2014), available here (original source: Online Data Tables V and VIII at opportunityinsights.org/data).

Throughout, use \(N = 100{,}000\) families and set a random seed so that your results are reproducible.

Question 1: Transitory Income and Attenuation Bias

Let \(x_i\) be the parent’s permanent log income and \(Y_i\) the child’s permanent log income, with

\[ Y_i = \beta x_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad x_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2_p), \quad \varepsilon_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2_\varepsilon) \]

and \(\beta = 0.6\), \(\sigma^2_p = 0.3\), \(\sigma^2_\varepsilon = 0.3\). In each year \(t\) we observe parent income \(y_{p,it} = x_i + \nu_{it}\).

  1. Suppose \(\nu_{it} \sim N(0,\sigma^2_\nu)\) is i.i.d. across years, and let \(\bar{y}_{p,i}\) be the average of parent income over \(T\) years. Show that the OLS coefficient from regressing \(Y_i\) on \(\bar{y}_{p,i}\) satisfies \[\text{plim}\ \hat\beta = \beta\frac{\sigma^2_p}{\sigma^2_p + \sigma^2_\nu/T}.\] With \(\sigma^2_\nu = 0.3\), calculate the predicted estimate for \(T = 1\) and \(T=5\). (this question requires only math)

  2. Simulate the model with i.i.d. \(\nu_{it}\) (\(\sigma^2_\nu = 0.3\)) for \(T = 16\) years of parent income. Estimate the IGE using (i) one year of parent income and (ii) a 5-year average. Compare your estimates to the formula in part (a). (this question requires code)

  3. Now suppose the transitory component is persistent: \(\nu_{it} = \delta \nu_{it-1} + \sqrt{1-\delta^2}\,\sigma_\nu e_{it}\) with \(e_{it}\sim N(0,1)\), \(\delta = 0.5\), and \(\nu_{i1}\sim N(0,\sigma^2_\nu)\) (so that \(\text{var}(\nu_{it}) = \sigma^2_\nu\) in every year). Repeat part (b) and also estimate the IGE using all 16 years. Plot the estimated IGE against the number of years averaged (\(T = 1,\dots,16\)) for both the i.i.d. and the persistent case. Why is the bias larger when transitory shocks are persistent? (this question requires code)

Question 2: Life-Cycle Bias

Now suppose that the child’s log income at age \(a\) follows a heterogeneous income profile (as in Lecture 3):

\[ y_{ia} = Y_i + g_i (a - \bar{a}) + u_{ia}, \qquad g_i = \bar{g} + \kappa (Y_i - \mathbb{E}[Y_i]) + \xi_i\]

where \(\bar{a} = 40\), \(\bar{g} = 0.02\), \(\kappa = 0.025\), \(\xi_i \sim N(0, 0.01^2)\) and \(u_{ia}\sim N(0, 0.1)\) are i.i.d. and independent of everything else. Use the same \(x_i\) and \(Y_i\) as in Question 1.

  1. Interpret \(\kappa\). Why might it be positive? Show that if we regress \(y_{ia}\) on the parent’s permanent income \(x_i\), then \(\text{plim}\ \hat\beta_a = \beta\left[1 + \kappa(a - \bar{a})\right]\). (this question requires only math)

  2. Simulate child income for ages \(a = 25,\dots,55\). For each age, regress \(y_{ia}\) on \(x_i\) and plot the estimates against age, together with the formula from part (a) and the true \(\beta\). At what age is the estimate unbiased? (this question requires code)

  3. Combine both problems using the persistent transitory shocks from Question 1(c). Compute the IGE under two designs:

    • Design A (similar to Chetty et al. (2014)): child income is the average of ages 30 and 31; parent income is a 5-year average.
    • Design B (similar to the long-panel studies in Mazumder (2018)): child income is the average of ages 38 to 41; parent income is a 16-year average.

    Compare each estimate to the true \(\beta = 0.6\) and to what you would predict by combining the formulas above. (this question requires code)

Question 3: Relative and Absolute Mobility Across Commuting Zones

The data contain, for each CZ, absolute upward mobility (am, the expected income rank of children whose parents are at the 25th percentile of the national distribution), relative mobility (rm, the rank-rank slope), the probability of reaching the top quintile from the bottom quintile (p_q5_q1), the population in 2000 (pop2000), and a set of CZ characteristics.

  1. Load the data and drop CZs with missing mobility estimates. Plot am against rm and report the correlation between them, both unweighted and weighted by population (use weights(:pop2000)). (this question requires code)

  2. Because the rank-rank relationship is linear within each CZ, the expected rank of a child with parents at percentile \(p\) is \(\text{am} + (p - 25)\times\text{rm}\). For the 50 largest CZs, compute the expected rank at \(p = 10\) and \(p = 90\). List the 5 CZs with the highest expected rank at \(p=10\) and the 5 with the highest at \(p = 90\). Find a CZ that is in the top 10 for absolute upward mobility but has a rank-rank slope above the national average of 0.341. (this question requires code)

  3. Standardize all variables to have mean zero and standard deviation one. Regress am on each of the following one at a time, then all together: frac_commute_lt15, gini_bottom99, top1_share, social_capital, frac_single_mothers. Repeat the single-variable regression of rm on gini_bottom99 and top1_share (a within-U.S. “Great Gatsby curve”). Summarize what you find. (this question requires code)

Question 4: Interpretation

  1. In a few sentences, use your results from Questions 1 and 2 to explain why Chetty et al. (2014) estimate an IGE of 0.34 while Mazumder (2005) estimates about 0.6. Which other differences between the two studies (discussed in class) are not captured by your simulation?

  2. A policymaker sees your Question 3(c) regression and concludes that a policy which reduces the fraction of single-mother households by one standard deviation will raise upward mobility by the coefficient you found. Give two reasons this conclusion may be wrong, and explain how the movers design of Chetty and Hendren (2018) addresses one of them.
