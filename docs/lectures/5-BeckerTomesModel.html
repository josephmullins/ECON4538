Overview

  • We have learned that income inequality tends to persist across generations
  • Today: theory of intergenerational transmission
  • The Becker-Tomes model (Becker and Tomes 1979, 1986) is a workhorse framework
  • We’ll study some stylized versions
  • Key questions:
    • Why do earnings regress to the mean?
    • How do parents affect children’s outcomes?
    • What determines the speed of mobility?
    • Do the model’s predictions fit the data?
  • Building a unified framework for inequality and mobility

The Becker-Tomes Framework

Basic Setup

There are 5 basic ingredients in the model:

  1. In each period \(t\), one generation of adults raises the next generation for \(t+1\) (an “OLG” framework)
  2. Care only about consumption, with an altruistic weight \(\beta\) on future generations
  3. Can transfer wealth through:
    1. Financial assets \(A_{t}\), with rate of return \(r\); or
    2. Human capital, \(H_{t}\), with price \(w\) (the wage)
  4. A production function converts investments \(X_{t}\) into human capital \(H_{t+1}\)
  5. An ability endowment, \(z_{t}\), evolves exogenously.

A Simple Version

Here is a simple version that captures important forces:

  • Ability is mean-reverting: \[ z_{t+1} = \rho z_{t} + \eta_{t+1},\ \rho \in(0,1) \]
  • Human capital production: \[ H_{t+1} = \exp(\mu + z_{t})X_{t}^{\delta} \] where \(X_{t}\) has price \(\psi\)
  • Budget constraint: \[ C_{t} + \frac{A_{t+1}}{1+r} + \psi X_{t} \leq A_{t} + wH_{t} \]

A Recursive Formulation

Let \(\Omega_{t} = A_{t} + wH_{t}\) be total wealth in period \(t\):

\[ V(\Omega_{t},z_{t}) = \max_{C_{t},X_{t},A_{t+1}}\left\{ u(C_{t}) + \beta \mathbb{E}[V(\Omega_{t+1},z_{t+1})] \right\} \]

subject to:

\[ \begin{aligned} C_{t} + \psi X_{t} + \frac{A_{t+1}}{1+r} &\leq \Omega_{t} \\ \Omega_{t+1} &= A_{t+1} + wH_{t+1} \\ z_{t+1} &= \rho z_{t} + \eta_{t+1} \\ H_{t+1} &= \exp(\mu+z_{t})X_{t}^{\delta} \end{aligned} \]

Note: no constraints on borrowing (for now)

Model Solution

  • This is quite a simple model, but we can learn some interesting and useful things from it
  • We’ll show that investment does not depend on wealth or income
  • We’ll derive the IGE in terms of model primitives
  • We’ll look at the effect of innovations in \(z_{t}\) in the long-run

Result 3: The Long-Run Effects of Good Fortune

What is the long-run effect of an increase in \(z\)?

  • To do this, let’s assume that \(\beta(1+r) = 1\) for simplicity
  • We’ll take a derivative around the deterministic steady state
  • Assume \(u(\cdot) = \log(\cdot)\).

Result 3: The Long-Run Effects of Good Fortune

Exercise (board):

  1. Show that in general: \[ u'(C_{t}) = \beta(1+r)\mathbb{E}u'(C_{t+1}) \]
  2. Under our assumptions at steady state: \[C_{t}=C_{t+1}=\bar{C}\]
  3. Define \(Z_{t}=\exp(\mu+z_{t})\) and iterate the budget constraint forward (ruling out Ponzi schemes): \[ \sum_{t=0}^\infty \frac{1}{(1+r)^{t}} \bar{C} = \Omega_{0} + \sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(1+r)^t}\max_{X_{t}}\left[wZ_{t}X^{\delta}_{t}/(1+r) - \psi X_{t}\right] \]

Result 3: The Long-Run Effects of Good Fortune

  1. Show that \[ \begin{aligned} &\max_{X_{t}}\left[wZ_{t}X^{\delta}_{t}/(1+r) - \psi X_{t}\right] \\ &\qquad = \left(\frac{Z_{t}w}{(1+r)}\right)^{1/(1-\delta)}\psi^{-\delta/(1-\delta)}\left(\delta^{\delta/(1-\delta)} - \delta^{1/(1-\delta)}\right) \end{aligned}\]
  2. Hence, since \(\sum_{t}(1+r)^{-t} = (1+r)/r\): \[ \frac{1+r}{r}\bar{C} = \Omega_{0} + B\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\frac{\exp(z_{t})^{1/(1-\delta)}}{(1+r)^{t}} \quad \left(= \Omega_{0}+B\frac{1+r}{r} \text{ when } z_{t}=0\ \forall t\right)\] where \(B\) collects constants in previous expression.

Result 3: The Long-Run Effects of Good Fortune

  1. Consider a one-time, small increase in \(z_{0}\), s.t. \(z_{t} = \rho^{t}z_{0}\): \[ \frac{d\bar{C}}{dz_{0}}\Big\vert_{z_{0}=0} = \frac{r}{1+r} \frac{1}{1-\delta} B\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\frac{\rho^{t}}{(1+r)^{t}} = \frac{1}{1-\delta}\times B\times \frac{r}{1+r-\rho}\]
  2. Dividing by \(\bar{C} = \frac{r}{1+r}\left(\Omega_{0} + B\frac{1+r}{r}\right)\): \[ \frac{d\log(\bar{C})}{dz_{0}}\Big\vert_{z_{0}=0} = \underbrace{\frac{B(1+r)/r}{\Omega_{0}+B(1+r)/r}}_{\text{human capital share of wealth}} \times\frac{1}{1-\delta}\times \frac{r}{1+r-\rho} \]
  3. Interpret! (Hint: what happens to \(\frac{r}{1+r-\rho}\) when \(\rho\rightarrow 1\)? When \(\rho = 0\)?)

Connecting to IGE Estimation

Recall from last lecture we estimated:

\[y_{\text{child}} = \alpha + \beta \cdot y_{\text{parent}} + \varepsilon\]

  • In this model: \(\beta = \rho\) (ability persistence)
  • Independent of technology parameter \(\delta\)
  • Independent of prices \(w\), \(\psi\), \(r\)
  • Policy invariant: transfers don’t change IGE

Think: Is this consistent with geographic variation in mobility we saw?

Takeaways So Far

  • Investment does not depend on income (when borrowing is unconstrained)
  • Human capital differences fade away, but consumption effects last forever
  • Rich households use wealth bequests to maintain dynasties as human capital fades away
  • IGE determined by ability persistence (\(\rho\)) alone; \(\delta\) scales the level of inequality in \(\log(H)\) (by \(\frac{1}{1-\delta}\)) but not its persistence
  • But is this realistic? What about credit constraints?

A Two-Period Model

Why Start with Two Periods?

  • Simplify exposition of some key points
  • Two-period version captures key forces
  • Lessons generalize

Two-Period Setup

Parent’s problem in period 1:

\[\max_{C_1, C_2, X} u(C_1) + \beta u(C_2)\]

subject to:

\[ \begin{eqnarray} C_1 + \psi X + \frac{A'}{1+r} &\leq& \Omega \\ C_2 &=& A' + w z X^{\delta} \end{eqnarray} \]

  • \(\Omega = wH + A\): total lifetime wealth
  • \(A'\): bequests (can be negative = borrowing)
  • Notation change: from here on, \(z\) denotes the level of the child’s ability, i.e. \(z\) here plays the role of \(\exp(\mu+z_{t})\) in the infinite-horizon model

Combining Budget Constraints

Combining the two constraints:

\[C_1 + \frac{1}{1+r} C_2 \leq \Omega + \frac{w z X^{\delta}}{1+r} - \psi X\]

  • Left side: present value of consumption
  • Right side: wealth plus net human capital investment
  • Net investment: returns \(\frac{w z X^{\delta}}{1+r}\) but costs \(\psi X\)

First-Order Conditions

Euler equation:

\[u'(C_1) = \beta(1+r) u'(C_2)\]

FOC with respect to \(X\):

\[\psi = \frac{\delta w z X^{\delta-1}}{1+r}\]

Same result as before on optimal \(X\).

Investment Does Not Depend on Income (again)

Just as before, we get: \[X^* = \left( \frac{\delta w z}{\psi(1+r)} \right)^{\frac{1}{1-\delta}}\]

With Log Utility

If \(u(C) = \log(C)\), we can solve explicitly:

From Euler equation: \[\frac{C_2}{C_1} = \beta(1+r)\]

Therefore: \[(1+\beta)C_1 = \Omega + \frac{w z X^{*\delta}}{1+r} - \psi X^{*}\]

Where \[X^* = \left( \frac{\delta w z}{\psi(1+r)} \right)^{\frac{1}{1-\delta}}\]

Interpretation

What does this mean economically?

  • Parents optimize to equate marginal returns across uses
  • Rich and poor parents with same \(z\) invest the same in children
  • Differences in income absorbed by \(C_1\), \(C_2\), and \(A\)
  • Crucial assumption: can borrow against future returns to H.C. investment

Question: Is this realistic? What if parents can’t borrow?

Efficiency and the Social Planner

Is the Market Outcome Efficient?

Consider a social planner who maximizes weighted welfare:

\[\max_{\{C_{i1}, C_{i2}, X_i\}_{i\in[0,1]}} \int \left(\mu_{i1} u(C_{i1}) + \mu_{i2} u(C_{i2})\right)di\]

subject to resource constraint:

\[\int C_{i1} di + \frac{1}{1+r} \int C_{i2} di \leq \int \Omega_i di + \frac{w}{1+r} \int z_i X_i^{\delta} di - \psi \int X_i di\]

Key question: Does the planner’s solution coincide with the market?

Planner’s First-Order Conditions

The planner’s FOCs are:

\[\frac{u'(C_{i1})}{u'(C_{i2})} = (1+r)\frac{\mu_{i2}}{\mu_{i1}}, \quad \frac{\delta w z_i X_i^{\delta-1}}{1+r} = \psi\]

Compare to market FOCs:

\[\frac{u'(C_{i1})}{u'(C_{i2})} = \beta(1+r), \quad \frac{\delta w z_{i} X_{i}^{\delta-1}}{1+r} = \psi\]

  • Investment is identical: \(X_i^* = X_i^{planner}\)
  • Only difference: consumption allocation via weights \(\mu_{i1}, \mu_{i2}\)
  • Consumption FOCs coincide when \(\mu_{i2}/\mu_{i1} = \beta\) (and \(\mu_{i1}\propto 1/u'(C_{i1})\))
  • Market outcome is efficient for some choice of weights

Efficiency Interpretation

What does this mean?

  • The market outcome is Pareto efficient
  • “can’t make anyone better off without making someone worse off”
  • Relative to Laissez-Faire economy, Planner can redistribute but that’s it

But: This relies critically on perfect credit markets!

Incomplete Markets

Can Parents Borrow?

  • Is it really plausible that parents can borrow against returns on their investment in children?
    • And make the children pay for it?
  • No!!
  • All kinds of moral hazard problems + legal issues
  • Also implied no role for income in shaping human capital

A More Realistic Setup

\[\max_{C_1, C_2, X} u(C_1) + \beta u(C_2)\]

subject to:

\[ \begin{eqnarray} C_1 + \psi X + \frac{A'}{1+r} &\leq& \Omega \\ C_2 &=& A' + w z X^{\delta} \\ A' &\geq& 0 \end{eqnarray} \]

Positive bequests only!

Inefficient Investment

  • This now implies: \[ \frac{\delta w z X^{\delta - 1}}{1+r} \geq \psi \] with equality if \(A'>0\), and weakly greater (strictly greater when the constraint binds) if \(A'=0\).
  • Equilibrium is inefficient whenever some families are constrained.
  • Introduces a role for policy to improve market outcomes
  • Make everyone better off (Pareto improvement)

Investment When Constrained

  • Solve for \(X^*\) when \(A'=0\): \[ \max_{X} u(\Omega - \psi X) + \beta u(wzX^{\delta}) \]
  • With log utility: \[ X^* = \frac{1}{\psi}\frac{\beta\delta}{1+\beta\delta} \Omega \]
  • Note that \(X^{*}\) now depends on \(\Omega\)

When Do Constraints Bind?

The borrowing constraint binds if:

\[\frac{1}{\psi}\frac{\beta \delta }{1 + \beta \delta} \Omega \leq \left( \frac{\delta w z}{\psi(1+r)} \right)^{\frac{1}{1-\delta}}\]

  • More likely to bind when \(\Omega\) is low (poor families)
  • More likely to bind when \(z\) is high (high returns to investment)
  • Creates a region where investment increases with income

Investment and Income: Two Regimes

Note: the constrained slope \(\frac{\beta\delta}{\psi(1+\beta\delta)}\) does not depend on \(z\). But high-\(z\) children have a higher unconstrained target \(X^{*}\), so they are constrained over a wider range of wealth: losses from borrowing constraints are concentrated on high-ability children in poor families.

Part 1: The IGE does not depend on \(\delta\)

Code 
using Random, Statistics

# Infinite-horizon model without borrowing constraints
function simulate_dynasty(δ; ρ=0.4, σ_η=0.5, μ=0.0, w=1.0, ψ=1.0, r=1.0, N=10_000, T=20)
    Random.seed!(1234)
    z = σ_η / sqrt(1 - ρ^2) * randn(N)          # stationary distribution of ability
    logH = zeros(N, T)
    for t in 1:T-1
        X = @. (δ * w * exp(μ + z) / ((1 + r) * ψ))^(1 / (1 - δ))
        logH[:, t+1] = @. μ + z + δ * log(X)
        z = ρ * z + σ_η * randn(N)               # next generation's ability
    end
    return log(w) .+ logH                         # log earnings
end

ige(lp, lc) = cov(lp, lc) / var(lp)               # OLS slope

for δ in (0.2, 0.5, 0.8)
    logy = simulate_dynasty(δ)
    println("δ = $δ:  IGE = ", round(ige(logy[:, end-1], logy[:, end]), digits=3),
            ",  var(log earnings) = ", round(var(logy[:, end]), digits=2))
end
δ = 0.2:  IGE = 0.39,  var(log earnings) = 0.46
δ = 0.5:  IGE = 0.39,  var(log earnings) = 1.19
δ = 0.8:  IGE = 0.39,  var(log earnings) = 7.41

The IGE is \(\approx\rho = 0.4\) for every \(\delta\) (up to sampling error). \(\delta\) only scales inequality, since \(\mathbb{V}[\log(H)] = \mathbb{V}[z]/(1-\delta)^2\).

Part 2: Constraints Make the Relationship Concave

Code 
using Plots

# Two-period model with A' ≥ 0 and log utility (z now in levels: Z = exp(μ+z))
function investment_bt(Ω, Z; δ, β, r, w=1.0, ψ=1.0, constrained=true)
    Xstar = (δ * w * Z / ((1 + r) * ψ))^(1 / (1 - δ))   # unconstrained optimum
    Xc = β * δ / (ψ * (1 + β * δ)) * Ω                   # optimum if A' = 0
    return constrained ? min(Xc, Xstar) : Xstar
end

function simulate_bt(; δ=0.5, β=0.9, r=1.0, ρ=0.4, σ_η=0.5, μ=0.0, w=1.0,
                       constrained=true, N=20_000, T=30)
    Random.seed!(1234)
    z = σ_η / sqrt(1 - ρ^2) * randn(N)
    logy = zeros(N, T)
    for t in 1:T-1
        z = ρ * z + σ_η * randn(N)                       # child's ability
        Ω = w .* exp.(logy[:, t])                         # parent's wealth = earnings
        X = investment_bt.(Ω, exp.(μ .+ z); δ, β, r, w, constrained)
        logy[:, t+1] = @. log(w) + μ + z + δ * log(X)
    end
    return logy[:, end-1], logy[:, end]                   # (parent, child)
end

# average child outcome within 20 bins of parental earnings
function binned(lp, lc; nbins=20)
    edges = quantile(lp, range(0, 1, length=nbins + 1))
    bin = [clamp(searchsortedlast(edges, x), 1, nbins) for x in lp]
    return [mean(lp[bin .== b]) for b in 1:nbins], [mean(lc[bin .== b]) for b in 1:nbins]
end

pl = plot(xlabel="Log parent earnings", ylabel="Mean log child earnings",
          legend=:topleft, size=(900, 330), tickfontsize=11, guidefontsize=12,
          legendfontsize=11, left_margin=4Plots.mm, bottom_margin=4Plots.mm)
for (c, lab) in ((false, "No constraint"), (true, "A' ≥ 0"))
    lp, lc = simulate_bt(constrained=c)
    lo = lp .< median(lp)
    println(lab, ": IGE = ", round(ige(lp, lc), digits=2),
            ", below median = ", round(ige(lp[lo], lc[lo]), digits=2),
            ", above median = ", round(ige(lp[.!lo], lc[.!lo]), digits=2))
    bx, by = binned(lp, lc)
    scatter!(pl, bx, by .- mean(lc), label=lab, markersize=5)
end
pl
No constraint: IGE = 0.4, below median = 0.4, above median = 0.41
A' ≥ 0: IGE = 0.59, below median = 0.64, above median = 0.54

Constrained families pass on wealth shocks through investment, so the IGE is higher at the bottom. (Means are de-meaned to compare shapes.)

Why Is the IGE Higher for Constrained Families?

  • For a constrained family, \(\log(wH') = \log(w) + \log(z) + \delta\log\left(\frac{\beta\delta}{\psi(1+\beta\delta)}\right) + \delta\log(\Omega)\)
  • So \(\log\) child earnings depend on \(\log\) parental wealth directly (coefficient \(\delta\)), on top of the inherited ability channel (coefficient \(\approx\rho\))
  • For unconstrained families, only the ability channel operates
  • Prediction (Becker and Tomes 1986): the relationship between parent and child earnings is concave; earnings regress to the mean more slowly among poor families
  • Can we test this?

Does It Fit the Data?

Prediction 1: Concavity

“The direct relation between the earnings of parents and children … is likely to be concave rather than linear because obstacles to the self-financing investments in children decline as parents’ earnings increase.” (Becker and Tomes 1986)

  • Recall Chetty et al. (2014) from Lecture 4:
    • The rank-rank relationship is almost perfectly linear across the distribution
    • The log-log relationship is nonlinear, but it is flatter in the tails: restricting to parents between the 10th and 90th percentiles raises the IGE from 0.344 to 0.452
    • In levels, \(\mathbb{E}[Y_{c}|Y_{p}]\) is concave, but that happens mechanically even with a log-linear model, since \(\mathbb{E}[Y_{c}|Y_{p}] \propto Y_{p}^{\rho}\) for \(\rho<1\)
  • Little sign of a steeper relationship at the bottom of the distribution
  • What do you think? Does this rule out borrowing constraints?

Prediction 2: Persistence by Constraint Status

Mulligan (1999) derives nine predictions that distinguish Becker-Tomes from a purely “mechanical” (Galton) regression to the mean. A key test:

  • Split adult children (PSID, SCF) by whether they received, or expect, an inheritance of $25,000 or more
    • Group 1 (inheritance): parents very likely unconstrained; Group 2 (none): includes the constrained
  • Model predicts: earnings persistence higher in group 2; consumption should not regress to the mean in group 1 (coefficient of one)
  • Findings (of nine implications: four refuted, two verified, three mixed):
    • Earnings/wage persistence is not systematically higher in group 2 (OLS similar; IV estimates go both ways)
    • Consumption regresses to the mean even in group 1: a coefficient of one is rejected in most specifications

Mulligan’s Conclusion

  • “the observed intergenerational dynamics of measures of economic status are not the result of borrowing constraints” (Mulligan 1999)
  • But: constraints can exist without changing mobility much, e.g. if parents are unwilling to substitute consumption across generations, or if returns to investment are not very diminishing in the relevant range
  • A prediction that does hold: consumption is more persistent than earnings

The Goldberger Critique

  • Goldberger (1989): Does the economic model tell us anything a mechanical model of transmission does not?
    • Galton: \(y_{c} = \alpha + \beta y_{p} + \varepsilon\), regression to the mean with no optimization at all
    • Goldberger argued that the Becker-Tomes apparatus (altruism, investment, bequests) adds little testable content beyond such a reduced form
  • We have already seen a version of this: in the unconstrained model, the IGE is just \(\rho\), the persistence of ability. The earnings dynamics are observationally equivalent to Galton’s.
  • The economics only has bite through auxiliary assumptions (e.g. borrowing constraints), which is exactly what Mulligan (1999) sets out to test
  • What do you think? Why might a policymaker still prefer the economic model?

Taking Stock

  • The simple Becker-Tomes model with constraints makes sharp predictions about the shape of mobility
  • Those predictions find limited support in income data
  • Possible reasons:
    • Constraints may bind at specific ages (early childhood) that a two-period model cannot capture
    • Public schooling may relax constraints for some investments but not others
    • Investments are multidimensional (time, money, skills of different kinds)
  • This motivates the richer models in Lecture 7 (Caucutt and Lochner 2020; Lee and Seshadri 2019)

Policy

Policy Implications

  • Note that we would like to target transfers based on \(z\)
  • But \(z\) is unobservable
  • An alternative is income-targeted transfers
    • e.g. progressive taxation
  • Can also try direct public investment (e.g. education, early childhood programs)
  • The gains to society depend on the joint distribution of \((\Omega,z)\)
  • What does the evidence say?

What Does the Evidence Say?

In coming lectures we’ll see:

  • Evidence suggests borrowing constraints do bind for many families
  • Returns to college are high, but many don’t attend
  • Educational interventions (especially early childhood) show high returns
  • Suggests scope for welfare-improving policy
