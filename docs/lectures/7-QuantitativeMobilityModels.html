Quantitative Models of Intergenerational Mobility

Overview

  • The two-period Becker-Tomes model (Becker and Tomes 1979, 1986) gave us sharp intuition:
    • Without borrowing constraints, investment does not depend on income and the IGE is \(\rho\)
    • With constraints, poor families under-invest and the IGE rises
  • From Lecture 6 we know skills are produced over many stages of childhood (Cunha and Heckman 2007)
  • Today: put these two ideas together in a model we can solve and simulate
  • Then describe two influential quantitative papers: Caucutt and Lochner (2020) and Lee and Seshadri (2019)

What the Simple Model Can’t Do

  • One investment period: can’t separate early vs late childhood investment
  • Parents’ income is fixed: but young parents earn less than they will later
    • Constraints are likely to bind when children are young
  • No sense of magnitudes: how much would a $1,000 transfer change a child’s earnings? Does it matter when it is paid?
  • What do you think? If you had a fixed budget to transfer to low-income families, would you give it when the child is 3 or when the child is 13?

A Model of Early and Late Investment

Timing

A parent lives for two periods while the child grows up, then the child becomes an adult:

  • Period 1 (child is young): parent earns \(y_{1}\), invests \(I_{1}\), saves \(A_{2}\)
  • Period 2 (child is older): parent earns \(y_{2} = (1+g)y_{1}\), invests \(I_{2}\), leaves a bequest \(A'\geq 0\)
  • Period 3 (child is an adult): child has resources \(A' + wH'\)
  • Life-cycle income growth \(g>0\) means young parents would like to borrow against their own future income
  • Two borrowing constraints:
    • \(A_{2} \geq -\kappa y_{2}\): young parents can borrow at most a fraction \(\kappa\) of future income
    • \(A' \geq 0\): parents cannot borrow against the child’s future earnings (as in Lecture 5)

Technology

Child’s adult human capital: \[ H' = Z\theta^{\delta},\qquad \theta = \left[\gamma I_{1}^{\phi} + (1-\gamma)I_{2}^{\phi}\right]^{1/\phi} \]

  • \(Z = \exp(\mu + z)\): child’s ability, with \(z' = \rho z + \eta'\) as in Lecture 5
  • \(\theta\): skills at the end of childhood, produced by a CES technology
  • Mapping to Lecture 6: there, \(\theta_{t+1} = [\gamma_{1}I_{t}^{\phi} + \gamma_{2}\theta_{t}^{\phi}]^{1/\phi}\). Set \(\theta_{1} = I_{1}\) (early skills come from early investment), then \[\theta_{2} = \left[\gamma_{1}I_{2}^{\phi} + \gamma_{2}I_{1}^{\phi}\right]^{1/\phi}\] which is our \(\theta\) with \(\gamma = \gamma_{2}\) and \(1-\gamma = \gamma_{1}\) (normalizing \(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2}=1\))
  • Mapping to Lecture 5: \(I_{1} = I_{2} = X\) gives \(\theta = X\) and \(H' = ZX^{\delta}\) (Becker-Tomes)
  • \(\phi\) governs substitutability: elasticity of substitution \(1/(1-\phi)\)

Dynamic Complementarity

Exercise (board): Show that \[ \frac{\partial^2 H'}{\partial I_{1}\partial I_{2}} > 0 \iff \phi < \delta \]

  • Hint: \(\theta\) is homogeneous of degree one, so \(\frac{\theta\,\theta_{12}}{\theta_{1}\theta_{2}} = 1-\phi\) (one over the elasticity of substitution)
  • Interpretation: early investment raises the return to late investment as long as early and late investments are complementary enough relative to the diminishing returns \(\delta\)
  • We’ll use \(\phi = -0.5\) (elasticity of substitution \(= 2/3\)) and \(\delta = 0.6\)

The Parent’s Problem

\[\max\ \log(C_{1}) + \beta\log(C_{2}) + \beta^{2}\log(A' + wH')\]

subject to:

\[ \begin{aligned} C_{1} + \psi I_{1} + \frac{A_{2}}{1+r} &= y_{1} \\ C_{2} + \psi I_{2} + \frac{A'}{1+r} &= y_{2} + A_{2} \\ A_{2} \geq -\kappa y_{2}, &\quad A' \geq 0 \end{aligned} \]

As in Lecture 5, the parent values the child’s adult resources with log utility. A fully dynastic version would replace \(\log(A'+wH')\) with the child’s own value function.

Recursive Formulation

Work backwards. In period 2, the state is \((A_{2}, I_{1})\) (plus \(y_{2}\) and \(Z\)):

\[ \begin{aligned} V_{2}(A_{2}, I_{1}) &= \max_{C_{2}, I_{2}, A'\geq 0}\Big\{\log(C_{2}) + \beta\log(A' + wZ\theta(I_{1},I_{2})^{\delta})\Big\} \\ &\text{s.t.}\quad C_{2} + \psi I_{2} + \frac{A'}{1+r} = y_{2} + A_{2} \end{aligned}\]

In period 1, the state is \((y_{1}, Z)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} V_{1}(y_{1}, Z) &= \max_{C_{1}, I_{1}, A_{2}\geq -\kappa y_{2}}\Big\{\log(C_{1}) + \beta V_{2}(A_{2}, I_{1})\Big\} \\ &\text{s.t.}\quad C_{1} + \psi I_{1} + \frac{A_{2}}{1+r} = y_{1} \end{aligned}\]

  • Early investment \(I_{1}\) is a state variable in period 2: it changes the return to \(I_{2}\)
  • Dynasty: the child’s earnings \(wH'\) become next generation’s \(y_{1}\), and \(z' = \rho z + \eta'\)

The Unconstrained Benchmark

  • If both constraints are slack, the parent maximizes the present value of net returns: \[ \max_{I_{1},I_{2}} \frac{wZ\theta^{\delta}}{(1+r)^{2}} - \psi I_{1} - \frac{\psi I_{2}}{1+r} \]
  • Ratio of FOCs gives \[ \frac{I_{1}}{I_{2}} = \left(\frac{\gamma}{(1-\gamma)(1+r)}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\phi}} \]
  • Both investments are proportional to \(Z^{1/(1-\delta)}\), so \(\log(H') = c + \frac{1}{1-\delta}\log(Z)\) and the IGE is still \(\rho\)
  • Neither parental income nor the timing of income matters
  • With constraints, both matter. Now we need the computer.

Solving the Model

  • Each family’s problem is a small optimization problem over \((I_{1}, I_{2}, A_{2}, A')\)
  • Trick: given \((I_{1}, I_{2})\), what’s left is a consumption-savings problem with log utility, which we can solve by hand
    • Four cases: neither constraint binds, only \(A'\geq 0\) binds, only \(A_{2}\geq -\kappa y_{2}\) binds, both bind
    • Since the problem is concave, the case that satisfies the Kuhn-Tucker conditions is the solution
  • Then search over \(I_{1}\) (outer) and \(I_{2}\) (inner) with golden-section search
    • The inner search over \(I_{2}\) given \(I_{1}\) is exactly the period-2 problem \(V_{2}\)
  • Simulate dynasties for several generations to get a stationary distribution of \((y_{1}, Z)\)

Code: Model Primitives

Code 
using Random, Statistics, StatsPlots

Base.@kwdef struct Params
    β::Float64 = 0.9      # discount factor (one period ≈ 8-9 years)
    r::Float64 = 0.3      # interest rate per period
    w::Float64 = 1.0      # price of human capital
    ψ::Float64 = 1.0      # price of investment goods
    g::Float64 = 0.35     # parental income growth, period 1 to period 2
    δ::Float64 = 0.6      # returns to skills in H'
    γ::Float64 = 0.6      # weight on early investment
    φ::Float64 = -0.5     # CES parameter: elasticity of substitution = 1/(1-φ)
    κ::Float64 = 0.0      # young parents can borrow up to κ*y2
    μ::Float64 = 1.2      # mean log ability (a scale normalization)
    ρ::Float64 = 0.4      # persistence of log ability
    σ_η::Float64 = 0.4    # sd of ability innovations
end
# copy a Params with some fields changed, e.g. Params(p; κ=0.5)
Params(p::Params; kw...) = Params(; (f => getfield(p, f) for f in fieldnames(Params))..., kw...)

skills(I1, I2, p) = (p.γ * I1^p.φ + (1 - p.γ) * I2^p.φ)^(1 / p.φ)
humancap(I1, I2, Z, p) = Z * skills(I1, I2, p)^p.δ

# Given investments, solve the savings problem in closed form.
# R1, R2: resources net of investment; E: child's earnings; b: borrowing limit for young parent
function savings(R1, R2, E, b, p)
    (; β, r) = p
    val(C1, C2, C3) = (C1 > 0 && C2 > 0 && C3 > 0) ? log(C1) + β * log(C2) + β^2 * log(C3) : -Inf
    # Case 1: neither constraint binds
    C1 = (R1 + R2 / (1 + r) + E / (1 + r)^2) / (1 + β + β^2)
    C2, C3 = β * (1 + r) * C1, (β * (1 + r))^2 * C1
    A2 = (1 + r) * (R1 - C1); Ap = (1 + r) * (R2 + A2 - C2)
    (A2 >= -b && Ap >= 0) && return val(C1, C2, C3), A2, Ap
    # Case 2: only A' ≥ 0 binds
    C1 = (R1 + R2 / (1 + r)) / (1 + β); C2 = β * (1 + r) * C1
    A2 = (1 + r) * (R1 - C1)
    (A2 >= -b && E >= β * (1 + r) * C2) && return val(C1, C2, E), A2, 0.0
    # Case 3: only A2 ≥ -b binds
    C1 = R1 + b / (1 + r)
    C2 = (R2 - b + E / (1 + r)) / (1 + β); C3 = β * (1 + r) * C2
    Ap = (1 + r) * (R2 - b - C2)
    (Ap >= 0 && C2 >= β * (1 + r) * C1) && return val(C1, C2, C3), -b, Ap
    # Case 4: both bind
    return val(R1 + b / (1 + r), R2 - b, E), -b, 0.0
end

# golden-section search for the maximum of a concave function on [lo, hi]
function golden_max(f, lo, hi; tol=1e-7)
    c = (sqrt(5) - 1) / 2
    x1, x2 = hi - c * (hi - lo), lo + c * (hi - lo)
    f1, f2 = f(x1), f(x2)
    while hi - lo > tol * (1 + abs(lo) + abs(hi))
        if f1 < f2
            lo, x1, f1 = x1, x2, f2
            x2 = lo + c * (hi - lo); f2 = f(x2)
        else
            hi, x2, f2 = x2, x1, f1
            x1 = hi - c * (hi - lo); f1 = f(x1)
        end
    end
    x = (lo + hi) / 2
    return x, f(x)
end

Code: Solving and Simulating

Code 
# Solve one family's problem. T1, T2: government transfers in periods 1 and 2.
function solve_family(y1, Z, p; T1=0.0, T2=0.0)
    (; r, ψ, w, g, κ) = p
    R1, R2 = y1 + T1, (1 + g) * y1 + T2
    b = κ * (1 + g) * y1
    ϵ = 1e-10
    # period-2 problem: given I1, choose I2 (savings solved in closed form)
    function period2(I1)
        I2max = (R2 + (1 + r) * (R1 - ψ * I1)) / ψ
        return golden_max(I2 -> savings(R1 - ψ * I1, R2 - ψ * I2, w * humancap(I1, I2, Z, p), b, p)[1],
                          ϵ, I2max - ϵ)
    end
    I1, _ = golden_max(I1 -> period2(I1)[2], ϵ, (R1 + b / (1 + r)) / ψ - ϵ)
    I2, _ = period2(I1)
    H = humancap(I1, I2, Z, p)
    _, A2, Ap = savings(R1 - ψ * I1, R2 - ψ * I2, w * H, b, p)
    return (; I1, I2, H, A2, Ap,
              young_constrained = A2 <= -b + 1e-6, child_constrained = Ap <= 1e-6)
end

# Unconstrained benchmark (closed form)
function unconstrained(Z, p)
    (; r, ψ, w, δ, γ, φ) = p
    k = (γ / ((1 - γ) * (1 + r)))^(1 / (1 - φ))      # I1/I2
    θk = skills(k, 1.0, p)                           # θ when I2 = 1
    I2 = (w * Z * δ * θk^(δ - 1) * (1 - γ) * θk^(1 - φ) / ((1 + r) * ψ))^(1 / (1 - δ))
    return (; I1 = k * I2, I2)
end

# Simulate N dynasties for T generations; return the last parent-child cross-section
function simulate(p; N=3000, T=6, seed=1234)
    Random.seed!(seed)
    z = p.σ_η / sqrt(1 - p.ρ^2) * randn(N)
    y1 = [p.w * humancap(unconstrained(exp(p.μ + zi), p)..., exp(p.μ + zi), p) for zi in z]
    local out
    for t in 1:T
        zc = p.ρ * z + p.σ_η * randn(N)                       # child's ability
        sols = [solve_family(y1[i], exp(p.μ + zc[i]), p) for i in 1:N]
        out = (; y1 = copy(y1), Z = exp.(p.μ .+ zc), sols)
        y1 = [p.w * s.H for s in sols]                        # child becomes next parent
        z = zc
    end
    return out
end

ige(x, y) = cov(x, y) / var(x)
quartile_of(x) = [min(4, 1 + floor(Int, 4 * (sum(x .< xi) / length(x)))) for xi in x]

Policy Experiments

Early vs. Late Transfers

  • Give a transfer worth 10% of median income to one quartile of parents
  • Early: \(T_{1}\) paid in period 1, or Late: \(T_{2} = (1+r)T_{1}\) paid in period 2
  • Same present value, so an unconstrained family is indifferent and does not change investment at all
  • Compare the change in mean \(\log(H')\) for the children of recipients

Early vs. Late Transfers

Why Do Early Transfers Win?

  • Constraints bind when parents are young: income is low exactly when early investment is needed
  • An early transfer relaxes the binding constraint directly, so \(I_{1}\) rises
  • A late transfer arrives after \(I_{1}\) is sunk. With dynamic complementarity, a low \(I_{1}\) lowers the payoff to \(I_{2}\), so late money cannot fully make up for it
  • A late transfer can even lower \(I_{1}\): knowing more money is coming, the young parent wants to consume more today but cannot borrow
  • What do you think? What real-world programs look like “early transfers”? (Recall the EITC and cash transfer evidence coming in Lecture 8)

The Literature

Caucutt and Lochner (2020): Model

  • Our model is a stripped-down version of Caucutt and Lochner (2020)
  • Their model:
    • Dynastic altruism: parents value children’s utility, not just their resources
    • Parents invest in early and late childhood, with a CES technology allowing dynamic complementarity
    • Parents’ income rises over the life-cycle, and parents face borrowing constraints against both their own future income and their children’s
    • The technology is estimated from data on children’s skills and investment, and the model is matched to data on family income and investment

Caucutt and Lochner (2020): Lessons

  • Qualitative lessons:
    • Many young parents are constrained, and constrained families under-invest in both early and late periods
    • Because of complementarity, policies that relax constraints early tend to be more effective than late ones, and late subsidies are less effective for children who received little early investment
    • Effects persist across generations through the dynasty

Lee and Seshadri (2019): Model

  • Lee and Seshadri (2019) build a multigenerational life-cycle model with human capital accumulation:
    • Parents invest in children at several stages (early childhood, later childhood, college)
    • Adults continue to accumulate human capital over their working lives
    • Families can make financial transfers, and face borrowing constraints
    • Ability is partly inherited

Lee and Seshadri (2019): Sources of Persistence

  • Calibrated to U.S. data on intergenerational persistence in earnings, education, and wealth
  • Use the model to ask: what are the sources of persistence?
    • Inherited ability vs. parental investment in human capital vs. financial transfers
    • Persistence across more than two generations: grandparents can matter through the human capital and wealth they pass on, not just through \(\rho\)
  • What do you think? In the AR(1) model from Lecture 5, the grandparent-grandchild IGE would be \(\rho^{2}\). Why might a model with investment predict more persistence than that?

Why Build Quantitative Models?

  • Policy questions are about magnitudes and timing, not just signs
  • Reduced-form evidence (Lecture 8) tells us what a specific program did, in a specific place
  • A model lets us:
    • Compare policies that have never been tried
    • Account for general equilibrium and dynastic effects
    • Interpret why a program worked
  • The cost: results depend on functional forms and parameters, which must be disciplined by data (e.g. the technology estimates from Lecture 6)

Summary

  • Extending Becker-Tomes to early and late investment with life-cycle income changes the policy conclusions
  • Without constraints: investment, timing, and the IGE do not depend on parental income (IGE \(=\rho\))
  • With constraints that bind when parents are young:
    • Poor families under-invest early, and complementarity makes this hard to undo later
    • The IGE rises above \(\rho\), especially at the bottom
    • Early transfers raise children’s human capital more than late transfers of the same present value
  • Caucutt and Lochner (2020) and Lee and Seshadri (2019) take these models to the data to measure magnitudes
  • Next: what does the evidence from actual programs say?
