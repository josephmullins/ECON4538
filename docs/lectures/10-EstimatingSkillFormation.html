Connecting Theory with Evidence: Estimating the Technology of Skill Formation

  • But experiments alone do not tell us the technology \(f\)
    • Is early investment more productive than late investment?
    • How substitutable are investment and existing skills?
  • Today: how economists estimate the technology of skill formation

    2. Measurement systems and factor models
    3. Estimates: Cunha and Heckman (2008), Cunha, Heckman, and Schennach (2010), Attanasio, Meghir, and Nix (2020)

The Object of Interest

From lecture 6: \[ \theta_{t+1} = \left[\gamma_t I_{t}^{\phi_t} + (1-\gamma_t)\theta_{t}^{\phi_t}\right]^{1/\phi_t}e^{\eta_{t}} \]

  • \(\gamma_t\): productivity of investment at stage \(t\)
  • \(\phi_t\): governs substitutability between investment and current skills, \(\sigma_t = 1/(1-\phi_t)\)
  • As \(\phi_t \to 0\), Cobb-Douglas: \(\ln\theta_{t+1} = \gamma_t\ln I_t + (1-\gamma_t)\ln\theta_t + \eta_t\)
  • We want \(\gamma_t\) and \(\phi_t\) by age: that is what tells us when to invest and whom to target

Why Is This Hard?

Problem 1: Skills Are Latent

  • We never observe \(\theta_t\), only test scores and survey answers
  • Each measure is a noisy signal: \(M_{t} = \theta_{t} + \varepsilon_{t}\)
  • Same for investment: books in the home, trips to the museum, family income…
  • How noisy? In Cunha and Heckman (2008), the share of variance in each investment proxy that is due to the latent investment factor is between about 4% and 28%, depending on the measure and age
    • Most of the variation in “number of books” is not investment

Problem 2: Scales Are Arbitrary

  • A test score has no natural units
  • Any increasing transformation of a test score is an equally valid test score (Cunha and Heckman 2008)
    • Raw score, percentile, IRT score, \(\exp(\text{score})\)…
  • Changes in test scores (“value added”) are not invariant to these transformations
  • So is a gain from 40 to 50 “bigger” than a gain from 80 to 90? The data do not say

What do you think? Can you think of an outcome that does have natural units?

Problem 3: Investment Is Endogenous

  • Parents choose \(I_t\), and they observe things we do not
    • e.g., a child gets sick (\(\eta_t\) low) and parents invest more to compensate
    • or parents invest more in children who seem more able
  • Then \(I_t\) is correlated with \(\eta_t\) and OLS is biased
  • Plus there are many candidate input measures and few instruments

This is the same problem as in lecture 8: we cannot just regress outcomes on inputs.

Measurement Systems

A Measurement System

For each latent variable \(k\in\{C,N,I\}\) (cognitive, noncognitive, investment), several measures: \[ M^{k}_{j,t} = \mu^{k}_{j,t} + \alpha^{k}_{j,t}\ln\theta^{k}_{t} + \varepsilon^{k}_{j,t}, \quad j = 1,\ldots,m^k_t \]

  • \(\alpha^{k}_{j,t}\): factor loading (how strongly measure \(j\) reflects the skill)
  • \(\varepsilon^{k}_{j,t}\): measurement error, independent of \(\theta\) and of other errors
  • Normalization: set \(\alpha^k_{1,t} = 1\) and \(\mu^k_{1,t}=0\) (or \(E[\ln\theta^k_t]=0\))
    • The latent skill has no units, so we give it the units of measure 1

Identifying the Loadings

With two measures per period and \(\alpha_{1,t}=1\) (Cunha and Heckman 2008):

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Cov}(M_{1,t}, M_{1,t+1}) &= \text{Cov}(\theta_t,\theta_{t+1}) \\ \text{Cov}(M_{2,t}, M_{1,t+1}) &= \alpha_{2,t}\,\text{Cov}(\theta_t,\theta_{t+1}) \\ \text{Cov}(M_{1,t}, M_{2,t}) &= \alpha_{2,t}\,\text{Var}(\theta_t) \end{aligned} \]

  • Ratio of the second to the first gives \(\alpha_{2,t}\)
  • Then the third gives \(\text{Var}(\theta_t)\), and the measurement error variances follow
  • With classical errors, the full distribution of \(\theta\) is identified (Kotlarski’s theorem)

Why One Measure Is Not Enough

Suppose \(\ln\theta_{t+1} = a\ln\theta_t + \eta_t\) and we only have \(M_{1,t} = \ln\theta_t + \varepsilon_{1,t}\).

  • OLS of \(M_{1,t+1}\) on \(M_{1,t}\): \[ \text{plim}\ \hat a_{OLS} = a\cdot\frac{\text{Var}(\theta_t)}{\text{Var}(\theta_t)+\text{Var}(\varepsilon_{1,t})} < a \]
  • Attenuation bias: we understate self-productivity
  • Fix: use the second measure as an instrument \[ \hat a_{IV} = \frac{\text{Cov}(M_{1,t+1}, M_{2,t})}{\text{Cov}(M_{1,t}, M_{2,t})} \to \frac{a\,\alpha_{2,t}\text{Var}(\theta_t)}{\alpha_{2,t}\text{Var}(\theta_t)} = a \]
  • The loading \(\alpha_{2,t}\) cancels: the second test can be on any scale

Seeing It in a Simulation

Simulation Setup

  • Latent log skill \(\ln\theta_1 \sim N(0,1)\)
  • Parents invest more in higher-skill children: \(\ln I = 0.5\ln\theta_1 + \nu\)
  • Cobb-Douglas technology: \(\ln\theta_2 = 0.8\ln\theta_1 + 0.3\ln I + \eta\)
  • Two noisy tests for \(\theta_1\), two noisy proxies for \(I\), one noisy test for \(\theta_2\)
  • Compare:
    1. OLS using the latent variables (infeasible)
    2. OLS using the first noisy measures (naive)
    3. IV using the second measures as instruments

Simulation Code

Code 
using Random, Statistics, LinearAlgebra, StatsPlots

function simulate(N; a = 0.8, b = 0.3, σ_e = 1.0, λ = 0.8, seed = 1234)
    rng = MersenneTwister(seed)
    lθ1 = randn(rng, N)                                  # latent log skill
    lI  = 0.5 .* lθ1 .+ randn(rng, N)                     # endogenous-looking investment
    lθ2 = a .* lθ1 .+ b .* lI .+ 0.5 .* randn(rng, N)     # technology
    M1 = lθ1 .+ σ_e .* randn(rng, N)                      # test 1 (loading = 1)
    M2 = λ .* lθ1 .+ σ_e .* randn(rng, N)                 # test 2 (different scale)
    J1 = lI .+ σ_e .* randn(rng, N)                       # investment proxy 1
    J2 = 1.5 .* lI .+ σ_e .* randn(rng, N)                # investment proxy 2
    Y  = lθ2 .+ σ_e .* randn(rng, N)                      # noisy outcome test
    return (; lθ1, lI, lθ2, M1, M2, J1, J2, Y)
end

ols(y, X) = X \ y
iv(y, X, Z) = (Z' * X) \ (Z' * y)                        # just-identified IV

d = simulate(50_000)
c = ones(length(d.Y))
b_latent = ols(d.lθ2, [c d.lθ1 d.lI])
b_ols    = ols(d.Y, [c d.M1 d.J1])
b_iv     = iv(d.Y, [c d.M1 d.J1], [c d.M2 d.J2])
est = round.([b_latent[2:3] b_ols[2:3] b_iv[2:3]], digits = 3)
println("| | Latent | Naive OLS | IV |\n|---|---|---|---|")
for (k, name) in enumerate(["Persistence \$a\$", "Investment \$b\$"])
    println("| ", name, " | ", join(est[k, :], " | "), " |")
end

What About Substitutability?

Now generate data from a CES technology with \(\phi = -0.5\) (complements, \(\sigma \approx 0.67\)) and fit the CES by nonlinear least squares.

Code 
function ces_sim(N; γ = 0.4, φ = -0.5, σ_e = 0.7, seed = 7)
    rng = MersenneTwister(seed)
    lθ1 = randn(rng, N); lI = 0.5 .* lθ1 .+ randn(rng, N)
    lθ2 = (1/φ) .* log.(γ .* exp.(φ .* lI) .+ (1-γ) .* exp.(φ .* lθ1)) .+ 0.3 .* randn(rng, N)
    M1 = lθ1 .+ σ_e .* randn(rng, N); J1 = lI .+ σ_e .* randn(rng, N)
    return (; lθ1, lI, lθ2, M1, J1)
end
ces_pred(lθ, lI, γ, φ) = (1/φ) .* log.(γ .* exp.(φ .* lI) .+ (1-γ) .* exp.(φ .* lθ))
function fit_ces(y, lθ, lI)    # grid search for nonlinear least squares
    best = (Inf, 0.0, 0.0)
    for γ in 0.05:0.01:0.95, φ in -2.0:0.02:0.98
        abs(φ) < 1e-6 && continue
        ssr = sum(abs2, y .- ces_pred(lθ, lI, γ, φ))
        ssr < best[1] && (best = (ssr, γ, φ))
    end
    return (γ = best[2], φ = best[3], σ = round(1 / (1 - best[3]), digits = 2))
end
dc = ces_sim(5_000)
fits = (latent = fit_ces(dc.lθ2, dc.lθ1, dc.lI), noisy = fit_ces(dc.lθ2, dc.M1, dc.J1))
println("| Inputs | \$\\gamma\$ | \$\\phi\$ | \$\\sigma\$ |\n|---|---|---|---|")
for (name, r) in pairs(fits)
    println("| ", name, " | ", r.γ, " | ", r.φ, " | ", r.σ, " |")
end

With noisy inputs, the estimated \(\phi\) moves toward zero: we overstate how substitutable investment and skills are. Fixing this in a nonlinear model is much harder than IV, which is the contribution of Cunha, Heckman, and Schennach (2010).

Estimates

Cunha and Heckman (2008)

Cunha and Heckman (2008) estimate a linear (in logs) technology with two skills: \[ \ln\theta^{k}_{t+1} = \gamma^{k}_{1}\ln\theta^{N}_{t} + \gamma^{k}_{2}\ln\theta^{C}_{t} + \gamma^{k}_{3}\ln I_{t} + \eta^{k}_{t}, \quad k\in\{C,N\} \]

  • Data: Children of the NLSY79 (white males), ages 6–7 to 12–13
  • Measures: PIAT math and reading (cognitive), behavior problems subscores such as antisocial, anxious, hyperactive (noncognitive), HOME items such as books, museum trips, lessons, and family income (investment)
  • Instead of an ad hoc index of investment, they estimate the weights on each investment measure
  • To give skills meaningful units, they anchor them to adult outcomes: log earnings and high school graduation

Correcting for Measurement Error Matters

Self-productivity estimates (Cunha and Heckman 2008, Tables 2 and 16):

Factor model OLS on raw scores
Cognitive on lagged cognitive 0.977 0.591 (PIAT math)
Noncognitive on lagged noncognitive 0.884 0.643 (antisocial score)
  • OLS is strongly attenuated, just as in our simulation
  • OLS even gives a “perverse” negative effect of the HOME score on math

Cunha and Heckman (2008): Findings

  • Strong self-productivity of both skills
  • Cross-productivity: noncognitive skills help produce cognitive skills, but (in most specifications) not the other way around
  • Sensitive periods (stage-specific technology): the investment coefficient for
    • Cognitive skills: about 0.11 from ages 6–7 to 8–9, then about 0.04 in later transitions
    • Noncognitive skills: about 0.05, then 0.11, then 0.05
  • Sensitive periods for cognitive skills come earlier than for noncognitive skills
  • The relative effect of investment on the two skills depends on the metric (anchor) used

Cunha, Heckman, and Schennach (2010)

Cunha, Heckman, and Schennach (2010) estimate the nonlinear CES technology:

  • Two skills (cognitive, noncognitive), two stages of childhood, CNLSY data
  • Allow for measurement error in a nonlinear model (nonparametric identification of the factor distributions)
  • Allow investment to be endogenous
  • Anchor skills to adult outcomes
  • Key finding: substitutability between investment and current skills decreases in the later stage for cognitive skills, and is roughly constant (or slightly increasing) for noncognitive skills

Implications for Targeting

  • If \(\phi\) for cognitive skills falls with age, late remediation of cognitive gaps is expensive
  • So for children from disadvantaged families, the efficient policy invests relatively more early
  • Later remediation is more promising for noncognitive skills
    • e.g., mentoring and adolescent programs that work on motivation and behavior
  • This is the “no equity-efficiency trade-off” argument for early investment from lecture 6, now with estimates behind it

Attanasio, Meghir, and Nix (2020)

Attanasio, Meghir, and Nix (2020): human capital development in India

  • Data: Young Lives survey, children aged 1–12
  • Estimate production functions for cognition and health
  • Measurement system for skills and investments, as above
  • Treat parental investment as endogenous: model investment choices as depending on skills, resources, and local prices, and use variables that shift investment but not skills directly
  • Findings:
    • Parental investment affects cognitive development at all ages, but more for younger children
    • Investment affects health only at early ages

Handling Endogenous Investment

One common approach is a control function (a stylized version; \(w_t\) = household resources):

\[ \begin{aligned} \ln I_t &= \pi_1\ln\theta_t + \pi_2 \ln w_t + \pi_3 Z_t + \nu_t \quad &\text{(investment choice)} \\ \ln\theta_{t+1} &= f(\theta_t, I_t) + \rho\,\nu_t + \tilde\eta_t \quad &\text{(technology)} \end{aligned} \]

  • \(Z_t\): things that shift investment but do not enter the technology (e.g., prices)
  • Estimate \(\nu_t\) from the first equation, include it in the second
  • \(\rho \neq 0\) means investment responds to unobserved shocks
  • An RCT can play the role of \(Z_t\): in Colombia, Attanasio et al. (2020) use random assignment to the home-visiting program to shift investment

Identification Concerns

Normalizations Are Not Always Innocent

  • Factor models need normalizations: a loading of 1 and a location for each latent skill
  • Researchers often normalize every period (e.g., each skill has mean 0 and the first loading is 1 at every age)
  • Agostinelli and Wiswall (2025): in a dynamic model, re-normalizing every period is not innocuous
    • It restricts how the scale of skills can change over time
    • It can bias estimates of the technology toward particular functional forms
  • Freyberger (2021): a restriction that is a harmless normalization for one production function can be a substantive (and false) restriction for another
    • Normalizing in the initial period, or through anchoring, can then lead to misspecification

Scale and Anchoring

  • Because test scores have no units, statements like “investment at age 6 is twice as productive as at age 10” depend on the units of \(\theta\)
  • Anchoring (Cunha and Heckman 2008; Cunha, Heckman, and Schennach 2010): measure skills in units of an adult outcome (e.g., log earnings)
    • Then “productivity” means effect on adult earnings, which has meaning
  • But conclusions can still depend on which anchor is used
  • Lesson: read estimates of \(\gamma_t\) and \(\phi_t\) together with the normalization and anchor behind them

What Do We Learn from the Recent Work?

  • With more careful normalizations, Agostinelli and Wiswall (2025) find that investment around ages 5–6 is most productive for cognitive skills, especially for children with lower initial skills
  • Common themes across the literature:
    • Measurement error is large; correcting it changes conclusions
    • Investment matters more at younger ages, especially for cognitive skills
    • Experiments are valuable inputs to estimating the technology, not just program evaluations

Synthesis

Key Takeaways

  1. Estimating the technology of skill formation requires solving three problems: latent skills, arbitrary scales, and endogenous investment
  2. Multiple measures solve measurement error: a second measure works as an instrument; factor models generalize this
  3. Ignoring measurement error attenuates persistence and can distort the estimated substitutability
  4. Estimates point to strong self-productivity and earlier sensitive periods for cognitive than for noncognitive skills
  5. Substitutability for cognitive skills declines with age: invest early, especially for disadvantaged children
  6. Results depend on normalizations and anchors: these choices deserve as much scrutiny as the data
